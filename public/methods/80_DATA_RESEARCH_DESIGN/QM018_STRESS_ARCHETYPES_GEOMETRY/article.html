Stress Archetypes and Representative Scenario Geometry

Interpretable tail families, feasible representatives, and external relevance checks for scenario clouds

Data & Research Design
Advanced
A reusable framework for classifying adverse simulated scenarios into interpretable stress families and representing their cross-asset geometry without confusing generated frequency with calibrated event probability.

QM018 · Data & Research Design · Advanced

Core idea. Select an adverse subset of a joint scenario cloud, classify it with an explicit mutually exclusive taxonomy, and summarize each family with a feasible representative scenario in a standardized cross-asset geometry.

Use it for. Interpreting large scenario clouds, comparing the composition of modeled stress tails, selecting representative stress paths for review, and testing whether a taxonomy remains meaningful across seeds and historical stress episodes.

It does not establish. That a generated family share is the real-world probability of that event, that an average path is a feasible scenario, or that a taxonomy is adequate merely because every simulated path receives a label.

The Question

A joint scenario generator may produce thousands of future paths. Tail-risk metrics can summarize how severe the cloud is, but they do not necessarily explain what kinds of joint stress drive the tail.

A stress-archetype analysis asks four separate questions:

  1. Which scenarios are adverse enough to enter the tail set?
  2. Which interpretable stress family does each selected scenario belong to?
  3. What feasible joint scenario best represents each family?
  4. Do the family shares and representative geometries remain stable and externally relevant?

The method is descriptive and diagnostic. It turns a large tail cloud into an interpretable structure without converting simulated frequencies into unsupported event-probability claims.

Why It Matters

Stress testing is most useful when scenarios are severe, relevant, internally coherent, and interpretable for the risks being assessed (Basel Committee on Banking Supervision 2018). A raw Monte Carlo cloud can satisfy none of those communication goals by itself.

Two additional design problems arise in multi-asset work.

First, assets operate on different volatility scales. A 3% move can be extreme for one asset and ordinary for another. Raw Euclidean distance can therefore let high-volatility assets dominate scenario similarity.

Second, a synthetic mean path may be numerically smooth but economically impossible as an actual joint scenario. Scenario-reduction and medoid ideas provide a useful alternative: select a representative from the feasible scenario set rather than inventing a coordinate-wise average (Dupačová et al. 2003; Kaufman and Rousseeuw 1990).

Intuition

Suppose the adverse tail contains 500 twenty-day multi-asset paths. An analyst might want to know whether the tail is dominated by:

  • broad risk-off moves;
  • equity-led drawdowns with defensive assets holding up;
  • rate-led stress;
  • commodity-led inflation stress; or
  • patterns not covered by the named taxonomy.

The labels should come from explicit rules, not from a story written after inspecting the results. Once each path has exactly one label, family shares can summarize the composition of the generated tail.

The next step is geometric. Within a family, standardize asset moves by declared volatility scales and choose the actual simulated scenario closest to the family in that standardized space. That scenario is interpretable as a feasible representative—not as the expected path and not as a forecast of the next crisis.

The Method

1. Define the adverse scenario tail

Let \(X_m\) denote complete joint scenario \(m=1,\ldots,M\). Define a pre-specified severity functional

\[ S(X_m), \]

such as portfolio loss, maximum drawdown, a worst-window return transformed to a loss orientation, or another application-specific stress statistic.

For a quantile-based tail with threshold \(q_\tau\), define

\[ \mathcal T_\tau = \{m:S(X_m)\ge q_\tau\}. \]

The tail definition is part of the method specification. Changing the severity variable, quantile, portfolio, or tie rule can change the selected scenarios and therefore the apparent stress-family composition.

ImportantTail selection is not neutral

If the tail is selected using an equity-heavy portfolio, the selected cloud may naturally overrepresent equity-led stress. A taxonomy should therefore be interpreted conditional on the declared tail-selection rule.

2. Specify rule-based stress archetypes

Let \(A_1,\ldots,A_K\) be named stress families defined by observable scenario features. For example, a rule might use standardized terminal asset moves, peak-to-trough losses, or cross-asset sign patterns.

The public classification rule should be mutually exclusive and collectively exhaustive after adding a residual category. One transparent implementation is an ordered rule system:

\[ c(X_m)= \begin{cases} 1,&A_1(X_m)=1,\\ 2,&A_1(X_m)=0,\ A_2(X_m)=1,\\ \vdots\\ K,&A_1(X_m)=\cdots=A_{K-1}(X_m)=0,\ A_K(X_m)=1,\\ \text{Other},&\text{otherwise.} \end{cases} \]

If multiple raw rules can overlap, the precedence order must be fixed before examining which ordering produces more attractive results. Better still, design the predicates as explicitly disjoint when feasible.

The residual Other category is a diagnostic. A large or unstable Other share may indicate that the named taxonomy does not adequately span the generated stress shapes.

3. Compute scenario-family shares

For tail set \(\mathcal T\) of size \(N_T\), the generated share of family \(k\) is

\[ \widehat s_k = \frac{1}{N_T} \sum_{m\in\mathcal T} \mathbf 1\{c(X_m)=k\}. \]

The family shares, including Other, should sum to one.

WarningGenerated frequency is not calibrated event probability

\(\widehat s_k\) is a Monte Carlo frequency conditional on the scenario generator, forecast origin, tail-selection rule, and taxonomy. It is not automatically an estimate of the real-world probability that stress family \(k\) will occur. A probability claim requires separate calibration evidence linking generated frequencies to realized event frequencies.

4. Build a standardized cross-asset geometry

Choose a feature map

\[ g(X_m)\in\mathbb R^d \]

that represents the cross-asset stress geometry. A simple choice is the vector of cumulative asset returns over the horizon. Richer designs can append path features such as maximum drawdown, timing of trough, or worst-window return.

For asset or feature \(i\), define a location \(a_i\) and positive scale \(s_i>0\) using an admissible pre-specified estimation sample. The standardized geometry is

\[ z_{m,i} = \frac{g_i(X_m)-a_i}{s_i}. \]

A common stress interpretation uses \(a_i=0\) and \(s_i\) equal to an ex-ante volatility scale, so a unit move means approximately one volatility unit.

Volatility normalization prevents naturally high-volatility assets from dominating every distance calculation. It does not create a unique geometry: current-state volatility, long-run volatility, robust scale estimates, and covariance-whitened distances answer different questions. The chosen metric should therefore be documented and sensitivity-tested.

5. Select a representative feasible scenario

Within family \(k\), let \(\mathcal T_k\) be its set of selected scenario indices. A representative scenario can be chosen as the medoid under distance \(d(\cdot,\cdot)\):

\[ m_k^* = \arg\min_{m\in\mathcal T_k} \sum_{\ell\in\mathcal T_k} d(\mathbf z_m,\mathbf z_\ell). \]

The representative is

\[ X_{m_k^*}, \]

an actual member of the generated scenario cloud. This is analogous to medoid-based representation, where the representative object is one of the observed objects rather than a synthetic centroid (Kaufman and Rousseeuw 1990).

Scenario reduction similarly formalizes the idea of approximating a large scenario distribution with a smaller representative subset while controlling a distributional distance (Dupačová et al. 2003).

Representative actual scenario versus synthetic average path

A coordinate-wise average

\[ \bar X_k = \frac{1}{|\mathcal T_k|}\sum_{m\in\mathcal T_k}X_m \]

need not be an actual generated path. It may combine a day-one shock from one scenario with a recovery pattern from another and therefore describe no feasible joint realization in the cloud.

A medoid-style representative preserves feasibility. The trade-off is that it may not look as smooth or central in every coordinate as the average.

Validation Diagnostics

Simulation stability

A taxonomy that changes dramatically when only the random-number seed or simulation count changes may be too noisy for stable interpretation.

Useful checks include:

  • family-share dispersion across independent RNG seeds;
  • representative-scenario distance across seeds;
  • ranking stability of dominant families;
  • convergence as the number of simulated paths \(M\) increases; and
  • sensitivity to reasonable tail thresholds.

A fixed seed is useful for reproducibility, but seed reproducibility is not Monte Carlo stability. Stability requires repeating the analysis across seeds or larger simulation clouds.

Taxonomy adequacy

A rule set should be audited for more than label completeness. Useful diagnostics include:

  • raw rule overlap before exclusivity is imposed;
  • residual Other share;
  • very small named families that cannot be estimated stably;
  • within-family dispersion in standardized geometry; and
  • sensitivity of family assignments to small threshold changes.

A taxonomy can be exhaustive by construction yet economically poor if most scenarios fall into Other or if named families contain highly heterogeneous geometries.

Realized-family share

If historical realized stress windows are available, apply the same fixed taxonomy to those realized windows. For realized adverse windows \(Y_1,\ldots,Y_N\), define

\[ \widehat r_k = \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N\mathbf 1\{c(Y_j)=k\}. \]

Comparing \(\widehat r_k\) with generated \(\widehat s_k\) can reveal obvious composition mismatch. But this comparison should not be overinterpreted when the realized sample is small, overlapping, regime-concentrated, or selected by a different stress rule.

Top-k realized-family coverage

Let \(\mathcal K_k\) be the \(k\) most common named, non-residual families in the generated tail. A simple realized-family coverage statistic is

\[ \operatorname{Coverage}_k = \frac1N\sum_{j=1}^N \mathbf 1\{c(Y_j)\in\mathcal K_k\}. \]

This asks whether the generator’s dominant named stress families cover the taxonomy labels observed in historical adverse windows. It is a relevance diagnostic, not a test that those families have the correct probabilities.

Representative-geometry distance

Let \(\mathbf z_j^{\mathrm{real}}\) be a standardized geometry for realized stress window \(j\), using the same normalization convention as the simulated scenarios. Define distance to the closest generated representative as

\[ D_j = \min_k d(\mathbf z_j^{\mathrm{real}},\mathbf z_{m_k^*}). \]

Large distances indicate that the named representatives do not closely resemble that realized joint stress geometry under the chosen feature map and metric.

The absolute magnitude of \(D_j\) has no universal interpretation. It depends on the normalization, feature set, and distance metric, so comparisons should be made under a fixed design.

External Relevance and Leave-One-Period-Out Robustness

Historical stress episodes can inform whether a taxonomy and its representative geometry are externally relevant, but a retrospective match can be overstated if the same episodes helped design the rules, thresholds, scales, or representatives.

A leave-one-period-out or leave-one-year-out design can reduce this circularity:

  1. hold out one evaluation period or year;
  2. estimate any data-dependent normalization scales, thresholds, or representative-selection objects using the remaining admissible data;
  3. apply the frozen design to the held-out realized stress windows;
  4. record family assignment, top-k coverage, and representative distance; and
  5. repeat across held-out periods.

This does not create a large independent sample when crises are rare. It is a robustness check against one particular historical period dominating the geometry or taxonomy design.

If the archetype rules and normalization are fully ex ante and never estimated from realized evaluation episodes, leave-one-period-out recomputation may be unnecessary for those fixed components. The validation design should match the components that are actually data-dependent.

Common Failure Modes

Failure mode Why it fails Better practice
Calling generated family share an event probability Monte Carlo frequency is conditional on the generator and selection rule Say generated share unless external probability calibration is demonstrated
Allowing overlapping rules without fixed precedence Family shares become order-dependent Use disjoint rules or publish precedence
Dropping the residual category Uncovered structures disappear from the audit Retain Other and monitor its share
Using raw-return Euclidean distance High-volatility assets can dominate geometry Standardize with a declared admissible scale
Calling an average path a representative actual scenario The average may not exist in the feasible cloud Select a medoid or another actual scenario
Using one RNG seed as stability evidence Reproducibility is not stability Repeat across seeds and simulation counts
Designing taxonomy from the same realized crises used for validation External relevance becomes circular Freeze rules ex ante or use leave-one-period-out checks
Treating top-k coverage as probability calibration Coverage measures label relevance, not frequency accuracy Separate coverage from calibration claims
